# Beyond the farm: transport, electricity and data centres

Food is one part of the climate problem; burning coal, oil and gas for power and transport is the larger part. To set diet and farming levers beside others, Planetshift also carries the fossil CO₂ of road vehicles, aviation, shipping and electricity, and the extra electricity of data centres, under each future scenario, with what it takes to act on each. `sectors.json` holds the data; the code is `pipeline/planetshift_pipeline/sectors.py`, with the AR6 scenario data read by `pipeline/planetshift_pipeline/sources/ar6db.py`.

These sectors are part of each scenario's fossil CO₂, not added to it: the scenario temperatures on the [temperature page](#method-climate) already include them. A lever takes a share of a sector's emissions away, and the change is turned into warming avoided with the same CO₂ response curve as the farm levers. The fuel no longer burnt also takes away the methane leaked producing it and the air pollution it made, as the farm levers count every gas they change: see *Methane and air pollution that come with the fuel* below. Data centres are the exception, described below: their growth is electricity demand the scenarios did not foresee, so it is added.

## The sectors and their futures

Each sector's emissions start in 2023 at a recent inventory and follow the shape of a scenario's trajectory from there. The scenarios are the three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways the app uses: SSP1-2.6 (strong mitigation), SSP2-4.5 (middle of the road) and SSP3-7.0 (regional rivalry, high emissions). For transport the shape comes from the scenario emissions FaIR itself is run with (RCMIP v5.1.0, the harmonised CMIP6 emissions); for electricity, from the same models' own results in the IPCC AR6 Scenario Database.

| Road, freight | 2.44 / 1.87 / 0.06 | 2.44 / 3.34 / 1.57 | 2.44 / 2.88 / 2.22 |
| Aviation | 0.95 / 0.91 / 0.56 | 0.95 / 1.47 / 1.80 | 0.95 / 1.81 / 3.00 |
| Shipping | 0.85 / 0.62 / 0.35 | 0.85 / 0.80 / 0.89 | 0.85 / 0.84 / 0.62 |
| Electricity | 14.15 / 4.53 / 0 | 14.15 / 13.07 / 0 | 14.15 / 23.71 / 40.55 |
| Data centres (extra) | 0 / 0.08 / 0 | 0 / 0.21 / 0 | 0 / 0.40 / 0.43 |

**Road transport** emitted about 6.1 billion tonnes in 2023, three quarters of all transport CO₂ (EDGAR's 2024 report puts transport at 8.24 billion tonnes). Passengers (cars, two- and three-wheelers, buses) take 60% and freight (trucks and vans) 40%, after the IEA's tracking of cars and vans and of trucks and buses; trucks and buses together are over a third of road CO₂. The shape is the scenario's surface transport, which also holds rail and domestic boats, a few percent of it. Under SSP1-2.6 road emissions fall to almost nothing by 2100 as vehicles electrify; under SSP2-4.5 they grow by a third to 2050 before falling; under SSP3-7.0 they stay high all century.

**Aviation**, domestic and international flights together, emitted about 0.95 billion tonnes in 2023 (IEA), back to its level before the pandemic. It is the hardest sector to clean up in every scenario: it holds roughly level under SSP1-2.6, nearly doubles under SSP2-4.5 and triples under SSP3-7.0.

**Shipping**, international and domestic, emitted about 0.85 billion tonnes (IEA, and the IMO's Fourth GHG Study for its split). The basis matters: the Fourth IMO GHG Study puts international shipping at 740 million tonnes in 2018 on a voyage basis, and all shipping, domestic and fishing included, at 1,056 million tonnes; 0.85 lies between, close to international and domestic navigation without fishing, whose fuel is counted with seafood here. It follows the scenarios' international shipping. The clean-shipping lever takes a share of it away (fuels without fossil carbon, such as green ammonia or methanol).

**Electricity** generation emitted 14.15 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2023, for 29,471 TWh of electricity demand (Ember, Global Electricity Review 2024), about two fifths of all fossil CO₂. Ember's 2025 review revises 2023 to 484 g/kWh and gives 14.6 billion tonnes for 30,856 TWh in 2024, 473 g/kWh. Heat sold by power stations (district heating), about another billion tonnes, is not included: the scenarios report it separately and no lever here acts on it. Under SSP1-2.6 coal and gas plants are replaced by wind, solar and other low-carbon sources, and emissions fall by two thirds by 2050. Under SSP2-4.5 they stay near today's level until mid-century, then fall. Under SSP3-7.0 they almost triple by 2100 as demand grows on coal.

## The grid's carbon intensity

The carbon intensity of electricity, kilograms of CO₂ per kWh generated, is the electricity CO₂ divided by the electricity generated, both from the same scenario run and each scaled to 2023's observed values, so the series starts at Ember's 0.48 kg/kWh for 2023 (CO₂ over demand, as Ember computes it).

| Scenario | 2023 | 2030 | 2040 | 2050 | 2070 | 2100 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SSP1-2.6 | 0.48 | 0.39 | 0.23 | 0.10 | 0 | 0 |
| SSP2-4.5 | 0.48 | 0.42 | 0.32 | 0.27 | 0.16 | 0 |
| SSP3-7.0 | 0.48 | 0.49 | 0.50 | 0.51 | 0.53 | 0.55 |

In SSP1-2.6, and in SSP2-4.5 at the very end of the century, the scenarios burn biomass in power stations and bury the CO₂ (bioenergy with carbon capture and storage), so the electricity sector's net emissions go below zero. Planetshift stops them at zero: the removals are a property of those plants, not something an extra kWh of demand would bring, and a negative intensity would make every electric car a carbon sink.

## The levers

Replacing a petrol car or a diesel truck by an electric one removes its tailpipe CO₂, but it now draws electricity, whose CO₂ depends on the grid. `evKwhPerKgCo2` is the electricity needed per kilogram of tailpipe CO₂ avoided, and the CO₂ an electric vehicle saves is its tailpipe CO₂ × (1 − `evKwhPerKgCo2` × the grid's intensity). So electric vehicles and renewable electricity work together: each makes the other worth more. Making the vehicles and their batteries is not counted. The fuels' CO₂ is the UK government's 2023 factor for 100% mineral fuel: 2.33 kg per litre of petrol and 2.63 kg per litre of diesel.

**Electric cars** act on road passengers' CO₂: 1.10 kWh per kg avoided (0.72–1.72). An electric car uses about 0.18 kWh per km at the plug (0.15–0.22, charging losses included), and the petrol car it replaces burns about 7 litres per 100 km (5.5–9). On today's world grid, 0.48 kg/kWh, an electric car saves about half of its petrol car's CO₂ (1 − 1.10 × 0.48 = 0.47); on a clean grid, almost all.

**Electric trucks** act on road freight's CO₂: 1.50 kWh per kg avoided (1.06–2.03). A 40-tonne battery truck uses about 1.3 kWh per km at the plug against 33 litres of diesel per 100 km; electric vans (0.25 kWh/km against 9 L/100 km) are the low end. On today's grid a truck saves about 30% of its CO₂ (1 − 1.50 × 0.48 = 0.28); on a grid of SSP3-7.0, where intensity rises, it saves little.

**Less flying** cuts aviation's CO₂ and, at once, its other effects. Aircraft also warm the climate through the contrails and the cirrus clouds they seed, the ozone their nitrogen oxides make (less the methane those oxides destroy), the water vapour they leave in the stratosphere, and soot and sulfate. Lee et al. (2021) assess the effective radiative forcing of these non-CO₂ effects in 2018 at 66.6 mW/m² (21–111, a 5–95% range), twice that of all the CO₂ aviation had emitted since 1940, from 1,034 million tonnes of aviation CO₂ that year. `aviationNonCo2` divides the two: 6.44 × 10⁻¹⁴ W/m² for each kilogram a year of aviation CO₂ (2.03–10.7 × 10⁻¹⁴). Unlike the CO₂, this forcing lasts only while the flying does (contrails for hours, the ozone for months), so it ends the year the flights stop and is turned into temperature with the forcing response below.

**Clean aviation fuel** (fuel made from waste, crops or captured CO₂ and hydrogen) cuts the CO₂ of the flights it fuels, but not their contrails: the planes still fly and still leave trails. Fuels with fewer aromatics make somewhat fewer ice crystals and weaker contrails, but that gain is uncertain and not counted. So clean fuel acts on the CO₂ part only, and less flying on both.

**Renewable electricity** takes a share of the electricity sector's CO₂ away, and with it the grid's intensity falls by the same share, which is what the electric vehicles and data centres then draw on.

**AI data centres.** Data centres used about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, 1.5% of the world's. The IEA's *Energy and AI* (2025) expects that to double to about 945 TWh by 2030 in its base case, mostly for AI, and to reach about 1,200 TWh in 2035; its Headwinds case reaches about 700 TWh and its Lift-Off case about 1,700 TWh in 2035. The scenarios were made around 2016, before that growth was foreseen, and already supply the electricity data centres used then; their demand had stayed roughly flat through the 2010s as efficiency kept up with use. So `dataCentresTwh` counts only the growth beyond 2024's 415 TWh as extra demand: 0 in 2024, 530 TWh in 2030 and 785 TWh from 2035 in the base case (Headwinds 155 and 285, Lift-Off 701 and 1,285), straight lines between the IEA's years and held flat after 2035, where the IEA stops. Its CO₂ is that electricity times each scenario's grid intensity, so it shrinks as the grid gets cleaner and the renewable-electricity lever cuts it too. The data-centre lever moves a share of this extra demand onto clean power. The emissions of building the data centres and making their chips are not included: published estimates are for single sites or companies and there is no world figure per TWh to build on.

## In the app

The **Energy and transport** fold of the Levers section has seven sliders, each the share adopted, ramping in from the current year to the year set by *All levers fully in place by*, like every lever (`app/src/levers/energy/model.ts`, a lever module called by `run.ts`). In each year *y*, with each adoption *a* already ramped and *g* the grid's intensity of the scenario chosen:

- Renewable electricity (`re`) cuts *a* × the electricity CO₂, and the grid left has the intensity *g′* = *g* × (1 − *a*).
- Electric cars (`ev`) and electric trucks (`et`) save *a* × road passenger (or freight) CO₂ × (1 − `evKwhPerKgCo2` × *g′*).
- Less flying (`fl`) and clean aviation fuel (`saf`) cut aviation CO₂ by 1 − (1 − *a*fly)(1 − *a*fuel); less flying also cuts aviation's non-CO₂ forcing by `aviationNonCo2` × *a*fly × aviation CO₂.
- Clean shipping (`cs`) cuts *a* × shipping CO₂.
- Each sector's change in CO₂, the electricity drawn counting in the electricity sector's, brings its co-emissions with it (below): its methane goes with the CO₂ into the emissions, its air pollution into a forcing and a methane of its own, counted under *air pollution*.
- Data centres on clean power (`cdc`): with the *AI data centres* option at its default, *growth added* (`dc=iea`), the extra demand's emissions go from its kWh × *g* in the baseline to its kWh × *g′* × (1 − *a*) in the scenario; with *as in the scenario* (`dc=ssp`) there is no extra demand, and the lever has nothing to act on.

The CO₂ and methane changes are convolved with their AGTP curves, the forcing changes with `forcingResponse`, and all are added to the warming change. The baseline, with `dc=iea`, also carries the data centres' extra CO₂ convolved with the CO₂ curve, from 2024. The likely range pairs the 95% AGTP and forcing response with the low end of `evKwhPerKgCo2`, the high end of `aviationNonCo2` and the high data-centre case (and the 5% with the opposite ends), so the low end of the warming avoided takes every range at its least favourable. In the summary's breakdown by lever, all of it counts as *Energy and transport*, on its own, split between *fossil fuels and industry* (CO₂, the fuel's methane, aviation's non-CO₂ forcing) and *air pollution* (the cooling lost and the methane NOx leaves); its CO₂ and methane are in the emissions avoided, and the forcings in the warming only. The farming band of the Causes of warming chart and the farm emissions are not changed by these levers.

The response is linear around the scenario, which holds well for small changes and less well when every lever is in full and most of a scenario's CO₂ goes: the figures are then a first-order estimate. The fold's *Adopt all in full* sets all seven at 100%.

## Methane and air pollution that come with the fuel

Burning coal, oil and gas emits more than CO₂. Producing the fuel leaks methane: coal mines vent it, oil fields flare and vent their gas, gas wells and pipes leak, together about a third of human methane. Burning it emits sulphur dioxide (SO₂), black carbon (soot), organic carbon and nitrogen oxides (NOx). SO₂ and organic carbon make particles that reflect sunlight and brighten clouds, so they cool; black carbon absorbs sunlight and darkens snow, so it warms; NOx makes ozone, which warms, and nitrate, which cools, and it shortens methane's lifetime, which cools more than its ozone warms. A lever that cuts fuel cuts all of these with the CO₂. `coemissions.json` holds what it needs; the code is `pipeline/planetshift_pipeline/coemissions.py`, the data read by `pipeline/planetshift_pipeline/sources/ceds.py`, and the model `app/src/levers/energy/coemissions.ts`, used by this module and by [Buildings and industry](#method-industry).

**How much comes with each kilogram of CO₂.** The Community Emissions Data System (CEDS, version v_2024_07_08, CC BY 4.0) gives the world's emissions of each species by sector and by fuel up to 2022. For each lever sector, its own ratio is what its fossil fuels emit of each species per kg of its fossil CO₂ (biomass, whose CO₂ is not fossil and which no lever replaces, left out): road for road passengers and freight alike, international and domestic aviation and shipping, power and heat plants for electricity, homes and commercial buildings for buildings, and the iron, steel, cement and lime plants, fuel and process, for industry. The supply ratio is what producing each fuel emits (CEDS's fugitive sectors: coal mining, oil, gas production and distribution) per kg of CO₂ from burning that fuel anywhere, shared by each sector's mix of coal, oil and gas. In 2022 producing a fuel leaked 3.6 g of methane per kg of CO₂ from burning coal, 4.8 g for oil and 5.1 g for gas, 131 million tonnes of methane in all (the IEA's Global Methane Tracker puts fossil fuels at about 120). Per kg of each sector's CO₂, own and supply together:

| Sector | Methane | SO₂ | Black carbon | Organic carbon | NOx |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Road | 5.0 g | 0.74 g | 0.19 g | 0.07 g | 4.8 g |
| Aviation (supply only) | 4.8 g | 0.40 g | 0.02 g | 0.005 g | 0.03 g |
| Shipping | 4.8 g | 4.5 g | 0.12 g | 0.08 g | 22.9 g |
| Electricity | 4.1 g | 2.3 g | 0.005 g | 0.006 g | 1.5 g |
| Buildings | 5.6 g | 1.1 g | 0.10 g | 0.12 g | 1.0 g |
| Steel and cement | 2.1 g | 1.4 g | 0.03 g | 0.04 g | 1.4 g |

Aviation's own NOx, sulphate and soot are already in its non-CO₂ forcing (Lee et al. 2021, above), so only its fuel's supply is counted here. Steel and cement have less supply methane per kg because much of their CO₂ comes from calcining limestone, which burns no fuel.

**How they change.** Each SSP has its own story for air quality: SSP1 cleans up fast, SSP3 hardly. Each ratio follows its scenario's ratio of the species to CO₂ in the matching RCMIP sector (transportation, aircraft, international shipping, the energy sector, residential and commercial, industry; the energy sector's methane against all fossil CO₂ for supply), relative to 2023. The ratio is kept from rising above today's, as emission factors do not get worse, and held once the sector's CO₂ falls below half of today's, where what is left of its pollutants is mostly not from fossil fuel (biomass, processes, bioenergy with capture) and the ratio stops measuring the fuel. Values are every five years from 2025, between them straight lines.

**What a kilogram does.** The forcing per kg of each species comes from FaIR's calibration (fair-calibrate 1.4.1, the 841 members), the same model as the temperature curves and the causes of warming: aerosol-radiation interaction for each species, aerosol-cloud interaction for SO₂, black and organic carbon, black carbon on snow and ice, and NOx's ozone. The aerosol-cloud effect is a logarithm of all the SO₂, black and organic carbon in the air, scale × log(1 + Σ shape × emissions): a kilogram matters less where the air is already dirty. The model takes its slope at the scenario's emissions of that year, so a lever's cut is valued at the margin of the scenario, linearly. Removing a quarter of the world's SO₂ at once, the slope is within 6% of the exact difference (a test checks it within 10%); a lever's cut is smaller. In SSP2-4.5 in 2023, the median forcing is −5.9 mW/m² per million tonnes a year of SO₂ (−11.1 to −1.8 across the members), +27 for black carbon (−123 to +65: FaIR's members disagree even on its sign once its cloud effect is counted), −6.9 for organic carbon and +1.2 for NOx. For each sector, the SO₂, black and organic carbon are added up member by member before the 5–95% range is taken, so the range keeps how the members trade one species against another; NOx stays apart.

**NOx and methane.** FaIR scales methane's lifetime by 1 + s × (E − E₁₇₅₀) for each NOx emission E, with s = −0.0034 per million tonnes of NO₂: less NOx, longer-lived methane. A kilogram less NOx therefore slows the removal of the methane above its pre-industrial level by −s / (1 + s × (E − E₁₇₅₀)) of itself, which acts as that much more methane emitted that year; the removal comes from a FaIR run of each scenario. In SSP2-4.5, a kilogram less NOx leaves 2.0 kg of methane in the air in 2023, 1.1 kg in 2100; in SSP1-2.6 1.7 falling to 0.4, in SSP3-7.0 2.6 rising to 4.0. That methane is turned into warming with methane's AGTP curve, and it outweighs NOx's ozone: in FaIR a tonne less NOx warms about as much, a few decades on, as a tonne less methane cools.

**Where the NOx is emitted.** FaIR's coefficient is one value for all the world's NOx, but a kilogram destroys more methane where the air is clean. Hoor et al. (2009, *Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics* 9, 3113, the QUANTIFY project) perturbed each transport sector's emissions in five chemistry models: per Tg of nitrogen, ships' NOx, emitted in the clean marine air of the tropics, gave a methane and methane-ozone forcing of −12.0 mW/m² against −3.0 for road's (Tables 1 and 8: −52.8 mW/m² from 4.39 Tg N, −20.6 from 6.85 Tg N), 4.0 times as much, 2.7 to 5.8 times across the models; road's figure is net of the carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons emitted with its NOx, which Planetshift does not count otherwise. Planetshift keeps FaIR's coefficient as the average over all the world's NOx, so the total, and the air-pollution band it is consistent with, do not change, and shares it between the sea and the land in that ratio: with ships a share *w* of each year's NOx in the scenario (15% in 2023, from RCMIP), land sources take 1 / (1 + 3.0 *w*) of the global coefficient and ships 4.0 times that. In SSP2-4.5 in 2023 that is 0.69 for land (0.58–0.80 across the models' ratios) and 2.75 for ships (2.2–3.4); the factors follow *w* each year, so they still average 1 (land 0.57 in 2100 in SSP2-4.5, whose ships' share rises, 0.96 in SSP1-2.6 and 0.89 in SSP3-7.0, whose ships' share falls). Road, power plants, buildings, industry and oil fields count as land, with no finer split for want of comparable multi-model figures for the sources other than transport; aviation's own NOx is in its non-CO₂ forcing. `noxCh4PerKgCo2` holds each sector's methane per kg of CO₂ cut, its NOx × its place's factor × FaIR's methane per kg of NOx, its range spanning both ranges. Ships on ammonia or methanol still burn fuel and make NOx from the air's nitrogen, so clean shipping keeps its NOx and cuts its fuel's sulphur, soot and supply; electric vehicles, heat pumps and renewables burn nothing and cut it all.

**Checked against FaIR.** A cut held from 2025 under SSP2-4.5 of 10 million tonnes of SO₂, 1 of black carbon or 10 of NOx, run through FaIR itself with 100 members, warms (SO₂, NOx) or cools (black carbon) by the same amount as this linear model, within a sixth in 2050 and 2100 (a quarter for black carbon, whose members spread so widely that a median of products is far from the product of medians); a test runs the comparison.

**In the model.** In each year every lever's change in fossil CO₂ is split by the sector that emits it, the electricity drawn by electric vehicles, heat pumps and clean industry counting as the electricity sector's on the grid left after renewable electricity. Each sector's change times its ratios of that year gives the change in methane, which joins the CO₂ in the emissions and is convolved with methane's AGTP under *fossil fuels and industry*, and the change in air pollution, whose forcing (each sector's particle forcing per kg of CO₂ plus NOx's) goes through `forcingResponse` and whose NOx methane, scaled for where it is emitted, goes through methane's AGTP, both under *air pollution*. The likely range takes each range's end in the direction of its band: more cooling lost and more NOx methane at the low end of the warming avoided. The Shapley breakdown and the causes add up as before, with air pollution a fourth cause the energy and industry levers act on; on the Causes of warming chart, its cooling band shrinks after today when they act.

Renewable electricity loses most at first, as coal power is the largest source of SO₂: 0.085 °C instead of 0.096 in 2050, and more in 2100 (0.253 against 0.234). Less flying, clean aviation fuel and clean heating gain from the start: their fuel's supply methane outweighs the little SO₂ they cut. Clean steel and cement lose most in proportion (0.012 instead of 0.025 in 2050): their plants emit SO₂ and the hydrogen route draws a lot of grid power. Electric vehicles gain a little: their soot and NOx's ozone go at once, and the methane their NOx no longer destroys, smaller on land, builds up over decades. With FaIR's one coefficient for every source they added 0.031 and 0.034 °C by 2100.

**Against published phase-outs.** Shindell and Smith (2019, *Nature* 573, 408–411) ran FaIR on the 42 pathways of the IPCC's 1.5 °C report, which cut fossil CO₂ and SO₂ by 75–85% by 2050. They find that "the temperature effects of CO₂, SO₂ and CH₄ reductions roughly balance one another through about 2040", after which the CO₂ cooling dominates; with all fossil-related emissions counted, any brief climate penalty is "no more than 0.05 °C" (0.19 °C at the 95th percentile), and with CO₂ and SO₂ alone 0.02–0.10 °C. Planetshift agrees: its levers in full, a faster phase-out (complete by 2035 for the sectors they cover), give no net warming in any year (+0.001 °C avoided in 2030 at the least), with the methane and CO₂ gains about equal to the cooling lost around 2030 and ahead of it after; the faster ramp brings the balance a decade earlier than in their pathways. Shindell and Smith count NOx's methane through FaIR's single coefficient; Planetshift shares it by where the NOx is emitted, which makes the land sectors' co-emissions a little more favourable and leaves the total unchanged.

## The temperature response to forcing

The levers on CO₂ are turned into temperature with the CO₂ curve of the [temperature page](#method-climate). Aviation's non-CO₂ effects and air pollution are a forcing, not an emission, so they need a different curve: `forcingResponse`, the temperature change after 1 W/m² of extra forcing held for one year, year by year for 200 years. It is made the same way as the AGTP curves: all 841 members of FaIR's calibrated ensemble are run on SSP2-4.5 as published and again with the extra forcing in 2035, and the difference is kept, with the median and the 5th and 95th percentiles. The extra forcing is added to FaIR's solar forcing, which acts on temperature with no chemistry or carbon cycle of its own, at the end of the year, where an emission made during the year first acts, so index 0 is the same calendar year as for the AGTP curves.

The median response is 0.068 °C in the year of the forcing (5–95% range 0.035–0.094), 0.107 °C the year after, the peak (0.078–0.144), 0.0089 °C after 10 years (0.0033–0.016) and 0.0016 °C after 50 years (0.0005–0.0041): most of the warming goes within a few years as the ocean's surface gives the heat up, and a small part, taken into the deep ocean, lingers for centuries. Added up over 200 years, the curve gives 0.65 °C per W/m², the warming from a forcing held that long. A forcing ten times larger gives the same response per W/m² to within 0.1% of the peak at every horizon (2.2% as a ratio, where the curve is small), so the response is proportional. As a check of size: aviation's non-CO₂ forcing of 2018, 0.067 W/m², held for 30 years would warm the world by about 0.03 °C.

## Assumptions

- Each sector's emissions are its scenario's shape scaled to a 2023 inventory: road 6.1, aviation 0.95, shipping 0.85 and electricity 14.15 billion tonnes of CO₂.
- Road passengers and freight split 60/40 in every year and scenario, both following the scenario's surface transport (road, rail and domestic boats together).
- Shipping, international and domestic, follows the scenario's international shipping.
- Electricity is generation only; heat from power stations is left out. Its CO₂ and the grid's intensity stop at zero where the scenario's bioenergy with carbon capture would make them negative.
- The scenario models are those CMIP6 used for each pathway (IMAGE for SSP1-2.6, MESSAGE-GLOBIOM for SSP2-4.5, AIM/CGE for SSP3-7.0), so electricity follows the same story as the emissions FaIR runs on.
- An electric vehicle needs 1.10 (car) or 1.50 (truck) kWh per kg of tailpipe CO₂ avoided, in every year; vehicles do not get more efficient over time, and making them is not counted.
- Aviation's non-CO₂ forcing is proportional to its CO₂ in the same year, at the 2018 ratio; clean fuel does not change it.
- Data centres add their electricity beyond 2024's 415 TWh, following the IEA's cases to 2035 and held flat after; building them is not counted.
- The response to forcing is FaIR's to solar forcing (efficacy 1) on SSP2-4.5 in 2035, used for every year and scenario.
- What comes with each kg of a sector's fossil CO₂ is CEDS's 2022 ratio, own fuel and fuel supply, changed with the scenario's emission factors, never above today's and held once the sector's CO₂ halves; road passengers and freight share road's ratio.
- Aviation's own NOx, sulphate and soot are in its non-CO₂ forcing; only its fuel's supply is counted as co-emissions. Clean shipping keeps its NOx.
- Air pollution's forcing per kg is FaIR's, the aerosol-cloud effect linearised at the scenario's emissions of each year; NOx counts its ozone, its nitrate and the methane it no longer destroys, that methane scaled for land (0.69 in 2023) and sea (2.75) by Hoor et al.'s ratio of 4.0 so that it averages FaIR's coefficient over each year's NOx.
- Carbon monoxide, volatile organic compounds and ammonia that come with the fuel are not counted (ammonia from fossil fuels is under 2% of the world's), nor refineries' and coke ovens' own pollution.
