# Carbon stored in farmland

Farming can take CO₂ out of the air as well as emit it. Plants put carbon into the soil, trees store it in wood, and some materials spread on fields store it. This page explains the four practices Planetshift offers as levers, how much CO₂ each takes up per hectare, and how much each could store worldwide. With each practice on the most land it can reach, that is about 0.37 Gt (billion tonnes) of CO₂ a year for cover crops, 1.3 Gt for trees on farms, 0.64 Gt for biochar and 0.17 Gt for rock dust, which is experimental.

## How the uptake is counted

For each practice, Planetshift needs four things:

- how much CO₂ a hectare takes up each year;
- on which land the practice can be used (cropland, or cropland and pasture);
- on how much of that land at most;
- for how many years the uptake lasts.

The uptake of a practice is its rate per hectare times the hectares where it is adopted. The lever sets how much of that maximum area adopts the practice, from 0 to 100%. The rate is a median with a likely range. For three practices, it depends on the climate.

| Practice | Land | Median (likely range), t CO₂/ha/yr | Most of the land | Years at that rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cover crops | cropland | 1.17 (0.6–2.0) in moist climates, 0.45 (0.2–0.8) in dry ones | 25% | 20 |
| Trees on farms (agroforestry) | cropland and pasture | 9.4 (4.7–18.7) tropical moist, 6.5 tropical dry, 4.3 (2.2–8.7) temperate moist, 3.0 temperate dry | 3% | 20 |
| Biochar | cropland | 1.9 (0.6–4.9) everywhere | 20% | 100 (no saturation) |
| Rock dust (enhanced weathering), experimental | cropland | 1.0 (0.2–1.8) in every climate | 10% | 100 (no saturation) |

The model's cropland is 1.69 billion hectares: the harvested area of the food crops, plus the oilcakes' land and temporary meadows (see [Land](#method-land)).

The practices fall into two kinds:

- **Cover crops and trees on farms fill a store that saturates.** The soil and the trees reach a new balance after some decades, so Planetshift counts their uptake for 20 years after adoption, then none.
- **Biochar and rock dust add carbon each year they are applied.** Their store keeps growing as long as the practice goes on, so their uptake is counted every year to 2100.

The rates are uncertain, and the ranges are wide. The reasons are given below.

## The four practices

Rates in the literature are often given in tonnes of carbon (t C). One tonne of carbon is 44/12 = 3.67 tonnes of CO₂. A petagram of carbon (Pg C) is a billion tonnes of carbon.

### Cover crops

Cover crops are plants sown between two main crops, in a winter or dry-season fallow, and left on or ploughed into the field. Their roots and residues add carbon to the soil.

The meta-analysis of Poeplau and Don (2015) found a mean gain of 0.32 t of carbon per hectare a year (1.17 t of CO₂). Its abstract names elevation and sampling depth, not climate, as the reasons field trials differ. Most of those trials are in moist climates. Lessmann et al. (2022) pooled meta-analyses by climate zone: cover-cropped rotations gain 0.14 t of carbon per hectare a year in temperate zones, 0.15 in subtropical and 0.17 in tropical ones, in the top 20–30 cm (their table 2). So one rate applies to every moist climate. In dry climates, water limits what a cover crop can grow. The IPCC's factor for high carbon inputs is 1.04 there, against 1.11 in moist climates, so the gain is about a third of the moist-climate gain.

Cover crops reach at most a quarter of cropland, the area implied by Poeplau and Don's global estimate: 0.12 Pg of carbon a year at their 0.32 t C per hectare is 375 million hectares, a quarter of the world's 1.5 billion hectares of cropland. At that cap, Planetshift's cover crops store about 0.37 Gt of CO₂ a year, 0.10 Pg of carbon. This is close to Poeplau and Don's global estimate (0.12 ± 0.03 Pg C a year).

### Trees on farms

Trees on farms (agroforestry) means planting trees in fields of crops (silvoarable systems, alley cropping) or in pastures (silvopasture). The trees store carbon in their wood and roots, and their litter adds to the soil.

Cardinael et al. (2018) synthesised 542 observations into rates by system and climate. Averaged over silvoarable systems and silvopasture, trees and soil take up about 8.6 t of CO₂ per hectare a year in temperate climates. In the moist tropics, where the synthesis has silvoarable systems only, they take up 18.7 t; its means for the whole tropics rest mostly on dry Asian plots. Those rates are for the plots studied, which have a median of 1250 trees per hectare and a median age of 8 to 11 years, and grow fast. Systems adopted on a large scale have fewer trees. The median is therefore half the synthesis' mean. The low is a quarter of it, and the high is the mean itself. In dry climates the rate is 0.7 times the moist rate, the IPCC's ratio of tree growth in the dry tropics to the moist tropics.

Planetshift takes 3% of cropland and pasture as a realistic maximum. On about 4.7 billion hectares of farmland, that comes to about 0.8 Gt of CO₂ a year. That is the economic potential the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report gives for agroforestry: 0.8 (0.4–1.1) Gt CO₂-equivalent a year at up to \$100 a tonne. Its technical potential is 4.1 (0.3–9.4).

### Biochar

Biochar is biomass (crop residues, manure, wood waste) heated without oxygen into a charcoal, then spread on fields. About two thirds of its mass is carbon. Most of it is very stable: 97% has a mean residence time of centuries in soil (Wang et al., 2016).

The rate assumes 1 t of biochar per hectare a year, which is what spare biomass can supply without clearing land for it. Each tonne holds 0.65 t of carbon, of which 80% remains after a hundred years. This is the IPCC's 2019 rule for counting it (2019 Refinement, volume 4, chapter 2, appendix 4: Table 4Ap.1 for the carbon content, 0.65 for herbaceous feedstock by pyrolysis, and Table 4Ap.2 for the share kept, 0.80 for char made at 450–600 °C). The rate is therefore:

$$
1\ \text{t} \times 0.65 \times 0.8 \times \frac{44}{12} = 1.9\ \text{t of CO}_2\text{ per hectare a year}
$$

The range spans 0.5 t of poorly carbonised char to 2 t of char made at high temperature. The rate does not depend on climate.

At that rate, the sustainable feedstock of Woolf et al. (2010) can supply at most a fifth of cropland. At that cap, biochar stores about 0.64 Gt of CO₂ a year. This is below the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report's potential for biochar (1.1, 0.3–1.8, Gt CO₂-equivalent a year at up to \$100 a tonne; 2.6 technically), so it is a conservative reading.

### Rock dust (experimental)

Rock dust (enhanced weathering) is crushed silicate rock, usually basalt, spread on fields. As it dissolves, it turns CO₂ from the air in the soil into bicarbonate. The bicarbonate drains to rivers and the ocean, where it stays for many thousands of years.

Rock dust is experimental. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report finds it demonstrated in the laboratory and in small field trials (technology readiness level 3–4), but not yet at scale (Working Group III, chapter 12). Planetshift offers it with rates set to what field trials have measured.

A trial on a Scottish grassland measured 0.26–0.41 t of CO₂ per hectare over about 1.5 years from 78 and 126 t of basalt a hectare, after taking off 22% for the CO₂ that degasses (McBride et al., 2025). That is about 0.2 t a year, the low end of the range, and 1–2% of what the rock could take up in theory. A four-year trial in the US Corn Belt measured 10.5 ± 3.8 t of CO₂ per hectare from 50 t of basalt a hectare each year (Beerling et al., 2024). That is 2.6 t a year. Grinding and hauling the rock can offset up to 30% of it on today's grids (Beerling et al., 2020, as the IPCC reports it). That leaves 1.8 t, the high end. The median in moist temperate croplands is 1 t, between the two. Weathering is faster where it is warm and wet, but Beerling et al. (2020), who model it with each nation's soils and climate, find that a tonne of basalt removes 0.17 to 0.20 t of CO₂ in every one of their twelve nations, from Canada and Poland to Brazil, Indonesia, India and Spain (Table 1, national removal over rock demand, all at 40 t a hectare). Every climate zone therefore takes the same rate.

Rock supply limits rock dust to at most a tenth of cropland. Over 3 billion tonnes of suitable crushed rock are produced each year (McBride et al., 2025, citing Renforth et al., 2011). Beerling et al. (2020) find that the main agricultural countries reach 0.5 Gt a year, net of the emissions of logistics, on about a tenth of their cropland, which already takes about 3.1 Gt of basalt a year at their 40 t a hectare (Table 1). 1 Gt takes about a quarter of their cropland and 5.9 Gt of rock. On a tenth of the model's cropland, rock dust stores about 0.17 Gt of CO₂ a year.

## Countries and climate

Where the evidence differs by climate, the rate is given for four zones: tropical or temperate, and moist or dry. Each country takes the zone of most of its cropland. The zones are in `removals_zones.csv`, set by hand from the IPCC's climate zones:

- tropical moist: Brazil, Indonesia and Nigeria;
- tropical dry: India, Mexico and the Sahel;
- temperate moist: the United States, China and Western Europe;
- temperate dry: Russia, Ukraine, Canada, Australia and Spain.

A country with no zone takes the `World` value. A large country spans several zones, so giving it one zone is a simplification.

## How long the uptake lasts

The number of years at the full rate (`years`) is read differently for the two kinds of practice.

**Cover crops and agroforestry fill a store that saturates.** Soil and trees reach a new equilibrium. The gain is fastest at first and slows as the stock approaches its new level. Planetshift counts the full rate for `years` after adoption, then zero. `years` is 20. That is the IPCC's default time to a new soil equilibrium, and about the age at which agroforestry trees are harvested or stop gaining much. The total stored is the rate times the years. Poeplau and Don's response function keeps gaining for over a century, ever more slowly, so 20 years is the conservative reading. When adoption ramps up, each year's new hectares start their own 20 years.

**Biochar and enhanced weathering accrue while applied.** Each year's char and rock add carbon that does not come back, so the stock keeps growing as long as the practice goes on. `years` is 100, meaning no saturation before 2100: the rate holds every year the practice is in place. When the practice stops, the uptake stops, but what was stored stays.

## Permanence

Carbon in soil and trees can be lost again. Several things release part of what was stored: ploughing up a field of cover crops, felling the trees of an agroforestry system, a drought, a fire, or a warmer climate that speeds up decomposition. Stopping the practice lets the soil drift back to its old level over decades. The rates here assume the practice is kept up. A model that stops the practice should release the stored carbon over about the same time it took to build up.

Biochar and weathering are far more permanent. Biochar loses a few percent in its first years and very little after. Bicarbonate in the ocean stays for millennia.

## Why the numbers are uncertain

The ranges are wide, for several reasons:

- Soil carbon is measured as a small difference between two large stocks, often only in the top 30 cm. Gains in the topsoil can be offset by losses deeper down.
- Field trials are made where the practice works well, and they are short. Extrapolating them to a whole country and over decades adds a large uncertainty.
- The agroforestry rates depend on how many trees are planted and how they are managed, which no global figure can capture.
- Enhanced weathering has few field trials, and some find slower uptake than models predict: the Scottish trial measured 1–2% of the theoretical uptake. How much of the dissolved carbon reaches the ocean is still being measured.
- Biochar's benefit depends on what the biomass would otherwise have done. When residues that would otherwise have rotted into the soil are made into biochar, the gain is smaller than the rate says.

These rates do not count side effects on yields or on nitrous oxide from soils. They also leave out the emissions of making the rock powder and the char, beyond the transport share for weathering.

The values, with one line of reasoning for each, are in `pipeline/removals.csv`. The code that expands them to countries is `pipeline/planetshift_pipeline/removals.py`, with the climate zone of each country in `pipeline/removals_zones.csv`. The result is `removals.json`.

## Assumptions

- Rates are per hectare of land where the practice is adopted, in tonnes of CO₂ a year, median and likely range.
- Cover crops: 0.32 t C/ha/yr in moist climates, a third of that gain in dry ones; at most a quarter of cropland; 20 years.
- Trees on farms: half the mean of Cardinael et al. (2018) for silvoarable and silvopastoral systems, trees and soil together; dry climates 0.7 of moist; at most 3% of farmland; 20 years.
- Biochar: 1 t/ha/yr, 0.65 t C/t, 80% kept after a century; at most a fifth of cropland; no saturation.
- Rock dust, experimental: 1 t CO₂/ha/yr net in moist temperate croplands, between the field trials in Scotland (about 0.2) and the US Corn Belt (1.8 net of logistics), the same in every climate; at most a tenth of cropland, limited by rock supply; no saturation.
- Each country takes one climate zone, that of most of its cropland.
- Practices are counted independently. Their uptakes on the same hectare are added, though a cover crop and biochar on one field may not add up in full.
